# Draw the neighbors

One illustration can be charming in isolation and exhausting when repeated. Review the *arrangement the rule produces*. A train of identical figures can be a deliberate joke. A row of identical faces can also hide the one expression the player needs to notice.

## Try the hard pair

Choose the two subjects that most need telling apart. Put them in their most ordinary poses and draw them touching or passing one another. Pick out the gap, overlap, stroke ending, posture or value mass that lets them remain individuals. Now try them in grayscale, at play size, halfway behind the same obstruction. Restore hue if it carries real information. If hue was the only clue and the rule demands a fast distinction, give that difference another shape.

## Draw a crowd

Fill the actual field with an ordinary distribution, including an awkward cluster. Circle the first three marks that catch the eye. If every face produces the same hard white flash, the brightest marks may be describing the brush preset. Vary them according to the surface, lighting or emotional emphasis that caused the mark. Give a quiet piece a silhouette with as much care as the hero.

Check the opposite failure too. A meticulous crowd can be the pleasure of a search game. Removing just enough detail to make its target obvious can erase the very reason to look around. See which boundaries help the eye return to a region after exploring it.

## Give a subject its performance

Cover the surroundings and play two actions. Can the subject alone convey what receives the force, resists, changes or survives? Restore the surroundings. A passing shadow, reflection or neighbor's glance may be the most eloquent part of the shot. Decide which of those two areas should carry the accent and settle the other one.
