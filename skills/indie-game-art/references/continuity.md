# Direct the spaces between actions

Take the last meaningful image before a change and the first useful action afterward. Ask what the player carries across that cut. It may be an object, unfinished business with another person, the place they should look next, or simply time to enjoy what they did. A cut can be brusque, gracious or funny on purpose. An identical dissolve at every boundary gives those moments the same emotional weight.

## Arrival, identity and waiting

The title can establish a relationship the game will develop. Someone waiting outside a locked room might hear music from within. Someone about to conduct an orchestra could enter to the sound of players tuning separately. Give returning players a direct path to play. If a menu already works, one well-observed gesture and a decisive composition may do more than redrawing the controls.

In a shared space, let arrivals change the group picture in a way the audience can follow. Keep identities and those waiting anchored. A gaze shift, a stool pulled out or an occupied space can communicate readiness. Names and readiness should survive a busy group, a late arrival and someone leaving. A solo game may use the same screen time for contemplating a loadout or a character. Preserve whichever activity gives waiting meaning.

At a transition, settle the action target under pointer, thumb or focus before enabling it. Hold the camera or layout steady across phase changes when players depend on the same spatial map. A deliberate reframe can introduce a new task. Return attention to a visible object after a menu closes. An incoming message can mark its own arrival without moving another player's view.

## Discovery, defeat and renewal

The image that ends a sequence can contain its history. In *Unpacking*, an alarm clock blanks while moved, then flashes twelve when set down. It is both a consequence and a memory of a recognizable object. In *Hades*, repeated escape attempts return the player to characters whose relationships develop. One effect works at the scale of a hand movement. The other asks for continuity across runs. Choose the timescale on which your players will value a change. [Witch Beam on the alarm clock](https://blog.playstation.com/2022/05/06/objects-tell-stories-in-unpacking-arriving-on-ps4-and-ps5-may-10/) · [Supergiant on Hades](https://www.supergiantgames.com/games/hades/)

- **Victory or discovery.** Hold on the specific thing achieved. A repaired stained-glass pane finally casts its complete image onto the floor. Let someone enjoy it, then offer the onward action where they can find it. If a score matters, keep the contributing event and the final total understandable after the flourish.
- **Failure or near miss.** Leave the cause visible long enough to understand when understanding is part of play. Allow a fast retry for a frequently repeated attempt. A narrative loss can call for an uncomfortable silence. Check how the scene feels on the fifth visit as well as the first.
- **Pause, disconnect or interrupted play.** Preserve place, the last reliable state and the next safe action. A reconnecting player may have missed the storytelling beat. Show enough of its consequence to reorient them, with whatever private information their role permits.
- **No conventional result screen.** In a sandbox, room-scale experience or open-ended story, visiting again may be the payoff. Find what remains in the world and how someone can recognize their own part in it.

For a full-game review, walk a player from arrival through their first meaningful action and one consequential ending. Include the branch they are likely to see when tired, unlucky or interrupted. Compare the rhythm of the whole passage before polishing individual screens. For a one-screen brief, follow only the adjoining beats needed to understand it.
