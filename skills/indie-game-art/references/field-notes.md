# What a reference can decide

A game's reputation tells you little about what to draw next. These paired readings start with a disagreement an artist might actually have. The observations describe published work. The proposed edits and limits are our interpretation.

## Who gets to finish the picture?

In *Cuphead*, Studio MDHR inked moving drawings by hand and painted the level backgrounds in watercolor. In *Chicory*, the player is the one who brings color back to a coloring-book world. A beautifully painted backdrop could be a fine commission for the first game and consume the canvas promised to the player in the second. The distinction reaches all the way down to whether a given patch of ground should already be colored. [Studio MDHR on the ink and watercolor pipeline](https://blog.playstation.com/2020/07/28/a-great-slam-and-then-some-cuphead-is-out-today-on-ps4/) · [Chicory, publisher screenshots and description](https://finji.co/games/chicory/)

Take a small crop of your own scene and make a mark worthy of keeping even after you remove its grain, bevel or color grade. Try the same mark on a less glamorous neighbor. Then remove it from the place where the player will leave their own mark. If that space is sweeter empty, protect it when you commission the rest of the art.

## When should repeated subjects look alike?

Look at the cat-food tins in *A Little to the Left*. The similar outlines let you see the heights of the stacks immediately. The illustrations and colored bands on the labels invite a closer look within that family. In Afterburn’s *inbento*, a miniature target arrangement has to be compared with the food in the actual box. The salmon-pink piece carries curved bands and the yellow piece a tight coil. Their marks remain recognizable in both places. [Max Inferno’s cat-food still](https://images.squarespace-cdn.com/content/v1/635ffc7b70ac56311f9561a6/9357d313-19fd-4c34-b3c7-d3ecd749b80c/ALTTL-Catfoodcans.png) · [Afterburn’s full-size play frame](https://img.itch.zone/aW1nLzY1NTMzNDkucG5n/original/b%2F5OUT.png)

Try masking the outer contour, then the interior drawing. Which carries the rule, which carries the personality, and which should remain constant across the family? Where uniformity makes stacking or measurement legible, preserve it. In an ensemble where you follow individuals at a glance, you may want the reverse. Judge the little picture beside the big one as well.

## When should the interface take up space?

In *Papers, Please*, the mobile desk has to hold paper large enough to inspect. Lucas Pope shows experiments with a document carousel and a rack. Changing where papers can live also changes which discrepancies the player can see together. Stamping still asks for a distinct physical action. In *Slay the Spire*, Mega Crit describes dropping the demand to remember paragraphs about each enemy. An intent above each enemy puts the next threats on the same visual plane. Both designs economize, yet they protect different pleasures. [Pope's mobile prototypes and comparisons](https://dukope.com/devlogs/papers-please/mobile/) · [Mega Crit on communicating intent](https://arstechnica.com/video/watch/war-stories-slay-the-spire-war-stories/)

Try the decision on your scene. Put one consequential action in the player's hand. Put two things they must compare side by side. If the gesture makes the two things harder to compare, give up some physical ceremony. If handling evidence *is* the play, a faster generic confirmation can cost the game its central pleasure.

## When do words earn the frame?

In *A Highland Song*, Moira recalls asking her uncle questions. The choice of memory gives the player something to do in quiet company, even on a solitary walk. Inkle also shows how dialogue responds to walking away. The line has a speaker, a place and a possible interruption. Compare this with the intent experiment above. Repeated tactical reading and choosing what to remember put very different demands on a sentence. [Inkle on remembered questions](https://www.inklestudios.com/highland/2022/05/03/letters) · [Inkle on interruptible dialogue](https://www.inklestudios.com/highland/2023/05/11/autocut-for-responsive)

Cover a line for one turn. What disappears? It might be a warning, a relationship, a joke with a particular speaker, or only the third announcement of a fact already visible. Restore what the player would actually miss. Try the same line after the tenth repetition.

## Which part of a believable object can you invent?

In *Gorogoa*, Jason Roberts explains how scaffolding can both slow a falling rock and belong to a building under repair. He also describes the difficulty of a counterweight design that can solve itself or resist resetting. An ingenious visual connection can still be a poor repeatable control. In *INSIDE*, Playdead demonstrates a boy's shadow changing direction and scale under a moving light. The controlled camera makes a carefully staged illusion reliable. The visual explanation has to survive the views and actions the player actually has. [Roberts's illustrated design talk, especially slides 47 and 67–70](https://media.gdcvault.com/gdc2018/presentations/Roberts_Jason_Gorogoa_design_of.pdf#page=47) · [Playdead's lighting breakdown with moving examples](https://blog.playdead.com/articles/the_lighting_of_inside/lighting_of_inside.html)

Sketch the useful physical relationship before rendering the fixture. Give the player a second try, reverse the action, or view it from another supported angle. Watch where the contact, shadow or implied mechanism breaks. Sometimes one convincing contact mark survives every view and costs much less than a simulated assembly.

## Techniques borrowed from public art and game skills

- **Resolve a disagreement with a visual principle.** GameStudio's art-bible skill asks for rules that can settle competing choices and for a deliberate point of divergence from a reference. Try that on one asset before commissioning a style bible. A one-room project may only need two drawings to settle the matter. [GameStudio, pinned skill](https://github.com/bullish0x/GameStudio/blob/d844d8f3adb5be209c605aa48ad41efe331f78ae/.agents/skills/art-bible/SKILL.md#L76-L104)
- **Compare different layouts on a busy playable frame.** The game-ui-ux skill begins with the player's decision and moment of focus. Use that to test who owns an input or a fact. Some games deliberately ask the player to read dense instruments or long conversations. [game-ui-ux, pinned skill](https://github.com/parodyband/game-ui-ux/blob/f36ba36201ff210b365e4e8e8b0f184dae76f551/skills/game-ui-ux/SKILL.md#L49-L108)
- **Keep a stable drawing and identify what moves.** The concept-to-pixel-assets skill isolates moving parts against an approved source. A fixed bit of scenery beside the moving contour makes drift and a bad loop seam easier to spot. Choose dimensions and frame count for your own camera and rhythm. [concept-to-pixel-assets, pinned skill](https://github.com/ForestSenYT/concept-to-pixel-assets/blob/e3eb22824a8c402690f370ac32e9fd87dd05b82f/SKILL.md#L35-L71)
